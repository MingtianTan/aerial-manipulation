Aerial manipulation: keep the arm pretrained, learn flight separately

Reuse a vision-language-action model (VLA) that already knows how to use an arm, let a small Flight DiT learn flight from the scarce aerial data, and fine-tune the VLA to listen to it.

Part 1

Introduction

Problem

  • Aerial data is scarce. Tabletop manipulation data can be collected at scale. Aerial manipulation data needs expert teleoperation or hand-coded scripts and MPC, which is not enough to train a whole-vehicle policy from scratch.
  • The arm is already solved elsewhere. A VLA pretrained on tabletop data already has the language understanding and fine arm control. Scarce flight data should not be spent retraining it.
  • Don't edit the arm's actions with flight constraints. Writing flight feasibility into one shared action chunk changes the pretrained arm behaviour, and ties the flight coupling to that one backbone, so upgrading the VLA later means rebuilding the constraint.

Idea

  • Two policies. A small Flight DiT handles flight. A mature VLA (π0.5) handles the arm. Flight is learned by the small model; the VLA only learns to take the flight into account.
  • Flight DiT. A diffusion Transformer of about 170 M parameters (the size of RDT-170M), small enough to train on one desktop GPU. At every camera frame (20 Hz) it reads the last 0.2 s of the 120 Hz state, both images, the task and the chunk π0.5 is executing, and forecasts where the base will be over the next 2.5 s.
  • Arm VLA. The forecast reaches π0.5 as Fflight: 11 tokens placed in front of the action tokens of its action expert, which π0.5's VLM never sees. π0.5 keeps the 8-D end-effector chunks of the 30k checkpoint (81.1 % in Part 5); its VLM and image encoder stay frozen, and only the action expert learns to read the tokens.
  • Two-way and two-rate. π0.5 re-plans every 0.4 s and reads the newest message; the Flight DiT ticks eight times in between and reads π0.5's current chunk with its age.
  • Easy to swap. The two policies meet only at the message. Replacing the arm VLA means re-training the message projections and the action expert on the new backbone; the Flight DiT and its data stay as they are.
  • One IK executes both. AM-Bench's floating-base IK, which produced every demonstration, turns π0.5's end-effector targets into base and arm targets; in a second configuration it also follows the Flight DiT's base path, so the faster loop acts on the vehicle directly.

Pipeline

  • Stage 0: labels. Flight labels come from the measured 120 Hz base path of the 4,800 demonstrations, with no re-recording. The IK holds base yaw fixed in every demonstration, so the forecast has no Δψ.
  • Stage A: flight only. Train the Flight DiT on all five conditions on one local GPU. It has to forecast the base better than constant velocity and than replaying π0.5's chunk through the IK before Stage B starts.
  • Stage B: teach π0.5 to read Fflight. Start from the 30k checkpoint, freeze the VLM and SigLIP, and train the action expert with the message in place, next to an identical run without it.
  • Stage C: deploy. π0.5 re-plans every 0.4 s, the Flight DiT ticks at 20 Hz, and one IK runs at 120 Hz.

Related work

Models

  • Transformer (Vaswani et al., NeurIPS 2017): the backbone of both policies. Attention is where language, images and Fflight reach the actions.
  • DiT (Peebles & Xie, ICCV 2023): a Transformer inside diffusion. The Flight DiT uses the same idea to generate the base path.
  • RDT-1B / RDT-170M (Liu et al., ICLR 2025): a diffusion Transformer robot policy. RDT-1B fine-tuning costs about a week on 4×A100; RDT-170M (hidden 1024, depth 14, 64-step prediction) sets the size of the Flight DiT, and its weights are one starting point.

Aerial manipulation

  • Flying Hand (He et al., 2025): an end-effector-centric interface on a fully actuated hexarotor with whole-body MPC. The high level only talks about the end effector. FA-Hexa is the same class of vehicle.
  • Aerial Manipulation in the Wild (Zhan et al., 2026): a Diffusion Policy proposes end-effector motion and whole-body MPC executes it, outdoors. Here the upper model is a tabletop-pretrained VLA instead.
  • π, But Make It Fly / AirVLA (Tucker et al., 2026): visual features transfer to flight, control dynamics do not. Their fix writes payload guidance into the VLA's sampling (23% → 50% pick-and-place). This project keeps the lesson but not the mechanism: flight is a separate module and only a condition.
  • AC-DiT (Chen et al., 2025): extract base motion first, then condition the body on it. Fflight comes from this idea, but AC-DiT still predicts the whole body with one network (a 170 M mobility head conditioning a larger whole-body head).

The sources of each part of the pipeline, and the reading list behind them, are in Develop mode.

  • Goal: ICML 2027. Dates are not out yet; going by the 2026 cycle, the abstract is due around 23 Jan and the full paper around 28 Jan 2027 (2026 dates). That is about 15 weeks from mid-October.
  • Done: the dataset. 4,800 verified demonstrations, 12 tasks × 5 conditions × 80, recorded with the upgraded specialists (Parts 2 and 3).
  • Done: the π0.5 baseline (Part 5). Fully fine-tuned on the 960 default demonstrations with both cameras: 81.1 % macro success on AM-Bench's twelve tasks against 49.2 % in the paper; robustness runs under way.
  • Next: Stage 0 and the Flight DiT (Part 4). Labels from the 120 Hz base path, Stage A on the local GPU, then Stage B: π0.5 with Fflight and a frozen VLM. A run in week 3, trained on the demonstrations' own future base path and tested with the scripted specialist's planned path, decides whether the channel is worth it.
  • Then: the benchmark (Part 5). ACT and Diffusion Policy as outside baselines, and π0.5 arms that differ in what reaches the action expert (no message, the Flight DiT's message, the raw body state, the body state in π0.5's text tokens), plus the message with all of π0.5 trained. Before / after

Both sides are FA-Hexa. Left: official AM-Bench code. Right: upgraded. All clips play together on a loop.

Toss ball

What changed

  • New specialist TossBallLobPolicy: carry the ball level to a hover 0.9 m behind the bin, where the IK holds the base, and run the end effector forward with the arm alone.
  • Predict the landing point from the ball's measured position and velocity; open the gripper when it reaches the bin centre. The ball leaves 0.6-0.7 m ahead of the base, beyond its collision disc.
  • New check (for the dataset): a ball that touches the robot after leaving the gripper fails the episode. The first version ran the whole vehicle and braked after the release; in wind the ball dropped onto the rotor arms and rolled into the bin.

Wipe window

What changed

  • New specialist WipeWindowPressPolicy: line up 6 cm in front of each stain, press straight in, hold 0.3 s, back straight out.
  • Measure the sponge's position in the hand before every press.
  • Skip stains that are already gone. Success check unchanged.

Frame assembly

What changed

  • New check: within 2 cm of flush against the wall, within 3 cm of the peg centre, under 10° to the wall, and still for 0.25 s.
  • New specialist FrameAssemblyBarGraspPolicy: roll the gripper 90° and clamp the top bar; approach from above so the frame is not knocked over.
  • Measure the frame in front of the pegs, align it, push it flush, release.
  • This task only: finger stiffness 100 → 1000, base x limit 1.0 → 1.25 m.

Open door

What changed

  • New check: the door starts latched and unlocks only after the gripper closes at the handle for 0.2 s.
  • New specialist OpenDoorHandlePolicy: align in closed loop in front of the handle, reach in, close, wait for the unlatch, then swing the leaf open on the handle in closed loop on the hinge angle to 22 deg, let go and back off. The check counts the door as opened only once the handle is released past 20 deg.
  • The first version swung the lever from a base that AM-Bench's IK kept 0.875 m behind the leaf, so the arm ran into its pitch limits as the handle moved away; under the thrust limits the outstretched arm saturated the rotors and the drone fell right after the success check. A second version pushed the leaf near the hinge instead. The final one lets the base stand 6 cm from the door frame, which keeps the arm within 0.83 m of reach at 22 deg; the clips show it.
  • Limit: the handle in door.usd is flush with the door and has no collision body, so it cannot be pinched. Closing at the handle stands in for operating it.

Peg in hole

What changed

  • At reset the fingers open to the object's width (width / 2 + 1 mm).
  • The object is seated against the palm instead of centred on the tool tip, so it is held deeper.
  • Specialist and check are official; 7 of 7 runs since.

Harvest lemon

What changed

  • New specialist LemonHarvestingCenterDropPolicy: the same pick, then the lemon itself is steered, using its measured position, to 6 cm above the container centre.
  • The gripper opens once the lemon is within 2 cm of the centre and nearly still.
  • Lands within 2 cm of the centre in 10 of 10 probe runs; the official drop lands 6–16 cm from the back rim. Success check unchanged.

Notes

  • Disturbances. AM-Bench offers thrust saturation, ground and near-wall effects, drag, a constant wind force and sensor/actuator noise, all off by default; no gusts, wind torque or payload changes.
  • π0.5 data. 80 demonstrations per task at 20 Hz in LeRobot format (EE camera, 8- or 12-value state, 50-step action chunks); AM-Bench fine-tunes all parameters, which needs more than 70 GB of GPU memory (LoRA: more than 22.5 GB).
  • AC-DiT data. RDT-170M mobility head plus RDT-1B whole-body head, trained on ManiSkill-HAB HDF5 at 20 Hz (three cameras, point cloud, 128-value state vector). An aerial base needs new state slots.

Full notes with file paths and sources: docs/research/disturbances-pi05-acdit-data.md.

Part 3

Dataset generation

Demonstration dataset

We record 80 successful demonstrations for each of the 12 AM-Bench tasks under each of the 5 conditions of Part 2 (AM-Bench default; thrust limits with aerodynamic effects; the same with a wind of 5, 10 or 20 % of the vehicle's weight), using the upgraded scripted specialists on FA-Hexa. Every episode is verified individually before it enters the training set. The set is exported for π0.5 fine-tuning in the OpenPI format and kept, with a 120 Hz low-dimensional companion, for the Flight DiT. All data was recorded in one day on a single RTX 4080 SUPER.

4,800episodes: 12 tasks × 5 conditions × 80
1.33 Mimage frames at 20 Hz, two cameras

Pipeline

  • Specialists. The Part 2 specialists, one per task, run closed loop on the measured object state (peg tip, handle, lemon, ball) and output an absolute end-effector target and a gripper command. Two Wind 20 % variants were added for the recording noise (PushSlider pushes the knob with the side of the closed gripper; WipeWindow aligns before a gentle press); RotateValve gets 30 s episodes under the disturbance conditions. The OpenDoor specialist was rewritten before the final recording: it unlatches the door through the lever, then slides toward the hinge and pushes the leaf open there in closed loop, instead of swinging the door on the lever from a base that the IK kept 0.875 m behind the leaf, which under the thrust limits saturated the rotors and made the drone fall right after the success check.
  • Simulation. Isaac Lab at 120 Hz with inverse kinematics and PID control; cameras render every sixth step, so a 20 Hz image is the exact render of the state it is paired with. Four environments per process, 20 m apart, two processes per GPU.
  • Recording. LeRobot v3.0, successful episodes only: a 20 Hz set with both 384 × 384 cameras (AV1 video), the 35-D state, the label and the executed action, and a 120 Hz set with state and labels at every control step. The task prompt is stored with every frame.
  • Verification and selection. Each episode is checked (below); the first 80 valid episodes per pair are selected and the rest excluded. The π0.5 export (LeRobot v2.1, 20 Hz, lossless PNG) is itself checked frame by frame against the selected source episodes.

Noise injection

Following DART, the executed target is perturbed and the specialist's unperturbed target is kept as the label, so the demonstrations contain small deviations and the corrections back to the nominal path. Three noise processes with the same standard deviation (5 mm, 0.025 rad; half of AM-Bench's defaults) behave very differently on a hovering vehicle (Figure 2). AM-Bench's per-step noise shakes the tool without moving the robot off its path, and every toss failed; an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck drift still changes the target every step, which the PID's derivative term turns into thrust spikes, and in 20 % wind the rotors saturate (press button 0 / 8). The smooth drift used here draws a new offset every 0.5 s and joins consecutive offsets with a smootherstep, so the target never jumps.

Verification

Every saved episode has to pass all of the following, and the two LeRobot sets are compared step by step. Rejected episodes stay on disk but are excluded from the selection and replaced by new recordings.

  • Success. The task's success check fired and every sub-criterion was true at termination (a lemon that reached the bin without a registered grasp is rejected).
  • Integrity. The 120 Hz episode has exactly the logged number of steps, the 20 Hz episode one frame per grid step, every video segment holds exactly its frames (LeRobot's streaming encoder silently drops a frame when its queue is full; the queue was enlarged after this was found), and the first, middle and last frames decode and differ.
  • Clean start. The robot does not move by itself in the first 0.2 s. In the first recording about a quarter of the OpenDoor episodes failed this: AM-Bench's IK keeps the base 0.875 m behind the door leaf, which for a door spawned close to the robot lies behind its reset position, so the base was pulled back on the first step. The rewritten door specialist sets its own base limit, and the transient no longer occurs.
  • Level base. The base never rolls or pitches beyond 25°, in every task. A drone that flips or falls into the object is not a demonstration even if the object still passed the success check; 23 such episodes were replaced.
  • Plausible labels. The target never runs more than 0.8 m past the usual extremes of its task–condition pair, and the end effector never gets more than 0.6 m farther from its target than usual. The first rule caught 8 default-condition lemon episodes whose target flew 2–5 m off after the lemon had slipped into the bin (the specialist kept steering it; now fixed), the second 11 episodes in 20 % wind in which the drone was thrown down to about 0.3 m above the floor before it recovered.
  • Length. Within 0.6–1.5× the median of its task–condition pair, which excludes late or accidental successes.
  • Metadata. The prompt, the disturbance switches and the wind force match the condition; the noise spread matches the setting.

Flight DiT, default-only run

A first, small test of Part 1's idea with what one workstation holds (two RTX 4090): does the system get better when π0.5 hands its action chunk to a Flight DiT? π0.5 is the frozen 30k checkpoint of Part 5. Only the Flight DiT is trained, and only on default-physics demonstrations. Run on 13–14 Nov 2026. Outcome on default physics: on held-out demonstrations the forecast is accurate (1.2 cm at 1.0 s) and reading the chunk lowers its error by 2–3 %; as a reference for the base in closed loop it loses two of the 11 tasks and leaves the other nine unchanged (judged successes: 271 of 330 for π0.5 alone, 227 and 229 with the Flight DiT). The same comparison under aerodynamics and wind is still running.

  • Data. 11 tasks (toss ball is left out), 200 demonstrations of the scripted specialist per task on default physics, recorded on 11–12 Nov 2026 with the noise and cameras of Part 3. 2,190 of the 2,200 pass Part 3's checks on length, tilt and labels; 8 per task are held out (88) and the other 2,102 train. The training data holds no rollout of π0.5, no thrust limit and no wind.
  • Models. The Flight DiT of Part 1 in two sizes, 44 M and 174 M parameters, each trained twice from the same seed: one model reads π0.5's chunk, its twin has the chunk masked. Both see the two cameras through a frozen DINOv2-S, the last 0.2 s of the 35-D state and the task, and learn the base's path over the next 2.5 s by flow matching. 50,000 steps, batch 256, peak learning rate 3·10−4 (the best of 5·10−5, 10−4 and 3·10−4 in a 10,000-step pilot), EMA 0.999.
  • Which chunk in training. π0.5 does not fly in a demonstration. In half of its samples the chunk-reading model therefore gets the chunk the 30k checkpoint computes offline from the demonstration's images, at every 8th frame (74,960 chunks; over their first 0.4 s they lie 0.7–1.9 cm from the specialist's targets, median per task). In the other half it gets the demonstration's own targets with a small random drift.
  • How it acts. π0.5 is not retrained here, so the forecast cannot reach it as Fflight. It acts through the IK instead (the base reference, M2, of Part 1's figure): at every step the anchor of the IK's base-smoothness term is moved towards the base position the Flight DiT forecasts a short lead time ahead, with a weight w. With w = 0 the controller is the one of Part 5. The base can follow the reference only where the arm leaves it free: its stand-off and height relative to the end-effector target.

Picked: w = 0.5, lead 0.1 s (22 of 40 judged successes; w = 0 on the same seeds 19 of 40, the other five settings 14 to 21). The strongest setting (w = 2, lead 0.2 s) took frame assembly from 7 to 1 of 10: the end-effector tracking error doubles (0.47 → 0.91 cm) and the frame is no longer placed in time. Neither lemon harvesting nor the inspection task was among the tuning tasks, and the setting was not changed after the scored rollouts.
  • Result. The base reference does not help, and it costs two tasks. π0.5 alone solves 271 of 330, with the chunk-reading Flight DiT 227 and with the chunk-blind one 229. The inspection task (NDT) falls from 30 of 30 to 0 and 0, lemon harvesting from 28 to 13 and 16. On the other nine tasks the columns are 213, 214 and 213 of 270: seed by seed, 14 of these rollouts succeed only with the chunk-reading Flight DiT and 13 only without it; the two runs of π0.5 alone, this column and Figure 4's, disagree on 31 of them. Whether the Flight DiT reads the chunk makes no difference to the outcome: 21 rollouts succeed only with the chunk-reading model, 23 only with the chunk-blind one.
  • The model's code or the reference? The Flight DiT runs inside the simulator's process. With it loaded and forecasting but w = 0, the IK is left as it is; on the scored seeds this gives 30 of 30 in lemon harvesting, 10 of 10 in the inspection task, and 6 and 8 of 10 in peg in hole (without the Flight DiT's code, three runs on these ten seeds: 7, 6 and 5). The two losses are the reference's doing.
  • What goes wrong in lemon harvesting. Once the lemon is picked the specialist's base backs away from the tree, and the Flight DiT forecasts that. The reference starts to move the base, the model now sees a base that is moving and forecasts that it moves on, and the reference follows the forecast. While the lemon is held the base runs back to x = 0.15 m and forward again, reaching 1.07 m/s (chunk-reading model; means over the rollouts). In the specialist's demonstrations it goes back to 0.56 m and reaches 0.45 m/s, with π0.5 alone 0.57 m and 0.43 m/s, and with the model loaded and w = 0, 0.57 m and 0.43 m/s. The end-effector tracking error in that phase is 1.35 cm instead of 0.49 cm, and in 12 of the 17 failures the lemon is picked but not delivered. On the same seeds the model's 1.0 s error is 3.2 cm while it only watches (w = 0) and 9.3 cm once it acts: it was trained on flights it did not influence.
  • What goes wrong in the inspection task. The mark is 7.0 m up, and the task counts when the end-effector stays within 8 cm of it (3 cm in depth) for 100 steps. Means over the rollouts: π0.5 alone reaches 1.55 m/s on the climb (the specialist 1.9 m/s), overshoots to 7.08 m and settles at 6.97 m. With the reference the climb reaches only 1.08 m/s and has no overshoot: the end-effector ends at 6.81 m, 19 cm from the mark (in no rollout does it pass 6.96 m), with the base 15 cm lower than without the reference. Which part of the loop lowers π0.5's targets has not been traced.
  • The forecast in closed loop. On the held-out demonstrations the 44 M chunk-reading model's 1.0 s error is 1.2 cm. On π0.5's own rollouts, the scored ones of its column, it is 5.7 cm, and the chunk-blind model's 6.1 cm in its column. On the chunk-reading column's rollouts a base that keeps its velocity is 11.3 cm off after 1.0 s and one that moves as π0.5's chunk says 9.1 cm; one that moves as the end-effector targets executed later do is 5.2 cm off, but those are not known in advance: 1.0 s ahead, π0.5's chunk is 5.4 cm from the target that is then executed. Reading the chunk lowers the model's error in the inspection task, frame assembly and cabinet pick and place (by 18, 17 and 12 %) and by less than 5 % either way in the other tasks. The Flight DiT has seen only the specialist's flights: in front of a door that π0.5 fails to open it keeps forecasting motion. In the second half of the failed open-door rollouts it has the base move 4.6 cm over the next second where it moves 1.3 cm; over the whole task its 1.0 s error is 4.8 cm, against 3.7 cm for a base that does not move.
  • Limits. This tests one way of using the forecast, a reference for the base inside the IK, with a model that has seen neither π0.5's flights nor a flight it influenced, nor a thrust limit, nor wind; it does not test the pipeline of Part 1, where π0.5 is retrained to read the forecast. The IK leaves the base free only in its stand-off and height. One training seed per model, a small tuning grid (40 rollouts per setting) on four tasks. The benchmark draws the size of the hole (peg in hole) and of the button (press button) once per evaluator process from the evaluator's seed: the scored cells of all three columns share one size, the tuning cells have another.

ACT-960, one policy per task, both cameras

ACT on the twelve tasks

  • Model. Action Chunking with Transformers (Zhao et al., 2023) as implemented in AM-Bench's LeRobot-based trainer, with the paper's hyperparameters (its Table 8a): ResNet-18 backbone pretrained on ImageNet, transformer width 512, 8 heads, feed-forward 3,200, 4 encoder and 1 decoder layers, KL weight 10, a chunk of 16 actions of which the first 8 are executed, one observation step. Inputs are the end-effector and body cameras at 384 × 384 (the same two views as our π0.5; AM-Bench's own ACT sees the end-effector camera only) and the 8-D proprioceptive state (end-effector position and quaternion, gripper width). Actions are 8-D end-effector targets relative to the current pose at 20 Hz, the representation π0.5 uses. One policy per task: twelve single-task models, not one multi-task model.
  • Data. The same 960 default-condition demonstrations as π0.5 (12 tasks × 80), exported per task to LeRobot v3 with lossless PNG frames (the recordings are AV1; the evaluator renders uncompressed), ImageNet image statistics and mean / standard-deviation normalisation of state and action.
  • Training. AdamW, learning rate 10−5 held constant for the transformer and the backbone, weight decay 10−4, batch 8, seed 1000, 20,000 steps with a checkpoint every 5,000, run on RunPod. The runs were then resumed exactly to 40,000 steps (optimizer moments, RNG state and step counter restored, constant learning rate), so 20k + 20k is one 40k run. 20k steps at batch 8 are about 8 epochs over 80 demonstrations; at 40k the short tasks have seen 22–25 epochs, frame assembly 7.6, the cabinet about 8.7 and lemon 13.8. (An ACT-4800 variant trained on all five conditions × 80 demonstrations to 20k steps, 1.5 epochs, is undertrained and not reported.)
  • Protocol. Identical to π0.5: AM-Bench's evaluator and success checks, default physics, 30 rollouts per task, rollout i with environment seed i, each task's own time limit, 20 Hz, one environment at a time, headless. Evaluation ran on an RTX PRO 6000 (Isaac Sim 5.1.0, Isaac Lab 2.3.2, LeRobot 0.4.4, eight environments in parallel); the cabinet task ran as three shards of ten seeds, which gives the same seed set as one run of thirty. Temporal ensembling

  • What changes. Only how the predicted chunks are executed; the weights are the same. AM-Bench's default runs the first 8 actions of each 16-step chunk open-loop and then predicts again. ACT's own inference scheme predicts a full chunk at every control step, decodes each chunk into absolute poses with the pose measured when that chunk was predicted, and executes the weighted mean of every chunk's prediction for the current step, with weights exp(−0.01·i) and i = 0 the oldest chunk. Positions and the gripper are averaged linearly; quaternions are sign-aligned to the oldest prediction, averaged and renormalised.
  • Effect. Seed-paired against the default execution on the same 40k checkpoints, both on the RTX PRO 6000: rotate valve 33 → 97 % (19 seeds succeed only with ensembling, none only without), wipe window 20 → 63 % (16 vs 3), pull lever 57 → 83 % (8 vs 0), harvest lemon 7 → 20 %; cabinet pick-and-place loses its four successes (0 vs 4). Open door does better with a shorter open-loop horizon instead: 4 executed steps per chunk give 87 % (26 of 30), against 40 % with 8 and 0 % with 1.

The same checkpoints, code, arguments and seeds give different per-task results on the RTX PRO 6000 (Blackwell, on RunPod) and on the RTX 4090 (Ada). Both machines run Isaac Sim 5.1 with a byte-identical patched evaluator. The two 40k + TE columns of Table 5 are this comparison.
