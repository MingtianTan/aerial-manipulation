The arm stays pretrained. Flight is only a condition.

Tabletop manipulation data is cheap to collect at scale. Aerial manipulation data is not. Reuse a VLA that already understands the arm, and let a small Flight DiT carry the scarce flight data.

01 — Problems try to solve

The data that teaches an arm does not teach a vehicle to fly.

Aerial manipulation has to stabilize an underactuated platform and still make contact with an arm. Language, vision, and grasping are already strong inside a fixed-base VLA. The flight dynamics are not in that pretraining, and the data that would put them there is the expensive kind.

01

Tabletop data scales. Aerial data does not.

Desktop manipulation can be collected in volume. Aerial manipulation data is expensive. It usually depends on expert teleoperation, or on scripts and MPC that hard-code the behavior. That supply cannot support learning a whole-vehicle policy from scratch.

02

Keep the arm the tabletop data already taught.

The reasonable route is to reuse a VLA pretrained on rich tabletop data, so the semantic understanding and the fine control of the arm stay intact. Scarce flight data should not be spent retraining the entire model.

03

Do not steer manipulation from inside one action chunk.

Physical constraints or guidance on a unified action chunk let flight feasibility intervene directly in how manipulation actions are generated. That can damage the pretrained VLA’s behavior distribution. It also welds the flight coupling to the backbone, so replacing or upgrading the VLA later means rebuilding the constraint, not just swapping a model.

02 — The idea

A light module owns flight. The mature VLA keeps the arm.

A small Mobility-to-Body Conditioning module models flight intent and injects it only as a condition. Flight data stays on the light module. The arm’s intelligence stays in the original VLA.

Meeting 025 put the same split this way: bring the DiT pipeline into the mobile base, other than a full-body transfer, which brings constraints and reduces the performance of the original pre-trained VLA.

Flight · a small Flight DiT

The conditioning module is a lightweight diffusion Transformer, in the spirit of AC-DiT’s mobility head, sized so that RDT-170M can move on one 4090. It reads language, multi-view images, and proprioception, emits a flight chunk, and leaves an intermediate token for the arm. The scarce flight data trains this module, not the large VLA.

  1. Action: aflight = [Δx, Δy, Δz, Δψ], horizon H
  2. Stage A supervises flight only
  3. The token before the MLP is Fflight, the flight intent

Arm · a mature VLA

π0.5 keeps the arm and the gripper. Most of its weights stay frozen. LoRA and a conditioning interface are the only new pieces. Flight intent is read at every denoising or flow-matching step. It does not rewrite the arm’s own action distribution.

  1. Action: aarm = [q1, q2, q3, q4, gripper], horizon H. FA-Hexa’s arm has three pitch joints and a wrist roll
  2. The interface is flight-to-arm conditioning
  3. Low level stays split: PX4 tracks flight, joint servos track the arm
Flight DiT Iterative denoising. Self-attention on the flight chunk. Cross-attention on the observations. This is the only place flight data has to land. a_flight , F_flight
Arm VLA Every generation step reads Fflight. The arm knows where the base is about to go, then chooses joints. Swap the VLA later and reconnect this interface. a_arm | F_obs , F_flight

The split is model-replacement friendly. Changing the arm backbone means reattaching the conditioning interface, not rebuilding the flight–arm coupling. AC-DiT’s mobility-to-body idea is the source of Fflight, but AC-DiT still emits the whole body from one network. Here the base and the arm are two policies. They share only the flight intent.

03 — The pipeline

Proposal pipeline

Inputs are a language instruction, forward / downward / wrist RGB, and pose, velocity, the four FA-Hexa arm joints, and the gripper. The vision-language encoder is the VLA backbone. Both policies emit a chunk of horizon H. Real-time chunking is optional.

Stage A · flight onlySupervise aflight and extract Fflight. Next week starts here.
Stage B · condition the armFreeze most of the VLA. Train the interface and LoRA against Fflight. This waits on Stage A.
This week · arm adaptationFrozen π0.5 does not yet know the task. Collect data and connect LoRA in sim first.
Stage C · deploySynchronize both chunks. PX4 tracks flight. Joint servos track the arm. Payload and safety filters stay optional.
04 — Related works

Related works

The model stack is a Transformer, a Diffusion Transformer, and RDT. The four aerial-manipulation papers under them are where that stack meets a flying arm. Quotations are from the papers.

A Transformer decides who looks at whom. Action tokens are the queries. Language, images, and later the flight intent are the keys and values. Cross-attention is the flight-to-arm interface: a condition, not a rewrite of the arm head.

DiT puts that Transformer inside a diffusion process, so a chunk can be drawn out of noise. In the derivation used here, a clean action x0 is noised into xt and the network learns the noise. Here x0 is the next H actions. For the Flight DiT those actions are the flight deltas. RDT is that idea trained as a robot policy. π0.5, the arm model already in the loop, generates with flow matching instead. Stage B only adds Fflight to the condition.

Forward noising

x_t = √ᾱ_t · x_0 + √(1 − ᾱ_t) · ε

ε ~ N(0, I). Training loss

L = || ε − ε_θ(x_t, t, c) ||²

c is language, images, and proprioception. After Stage B it also includes Fflight.

Transformer, DiT, and RDT

Vaswani, Shazeer, Parmar, Uszkoreit, Jones, Gomez, Kaiser, Polosukhin
NeurIPS 2017
Attention Is All You Need

The Transformer

We propose a new simple network architecture, the Transformer, based solely on attention mechanisms, dispensing with recurrence and convolutions entirely.

This is the backbone under both the flight module and the arm VLA. Self-attention reads the action chunk. Cross-attention is where language, images, and Fflight enter. Nothing in this project replaces that interface. Flight intent is just another condition on it.

Peebles, Xie
ICCV 2023 · arXiv:2212.09748
Scalable Diffusion Models with Transformers

DiT

We explore a new class of diffusion models based on the transformer architecture. We train latent diffusion models of images, replacing the commonly-used U-Net backbone with a transformer that operates on latent patches.

DiT swaps the U-Net for a Transformer inside diffusion. The Flight DiT keeps that swap and changes the target: patches of an image become a short flight-action chunk. The same scalability argument is why the flight module can stay small. Depth and width are chosen so RDT-170M fits on one 4090, instead of growing a second foundation model.

Liu, Wu, Li, Tan, Chen, Wang, Xu, Su, Zhu
ICLR 2025 · arXiv:2410.07864
RDT-1B · RDT-170M

RDT, the flight model that starts next week

RDT builds on diffusion models to effectively represent multi-modality, with innovative designs of a scalable Transformer to deal with the heterogeneity of multi-modal inputs and to capture the nonlinearity and high frequency of robotic data.
To address data scarcity, we further introduce a Physically Interpretable Unified Action Space, which can unify the action representations of various robots while preserving the physical meanings of original actions.

RDT-1B was pretrained on more than a million multi-robot episodes. Its unified action space packs end-effector, joint, velocity, and wheeled base quantities into one 128-dimensional vector. Fine-tuning the 1B model is about a week on 4×A100, or roughly $500 a run. That is too expensive for this project. RDT-170M is the small ablation: about 170M parameters, hidden size 1024, depth 14, half of RDT-1B on both axes. It takes language plus up to three RGB views and predicts 64 future actions. One 4090 and about a week. That is the checkpoint next week’s training starts from, sized as the light flight module rather than a second foundation model for the arm.

Aerial manipulation

He, Guo, Tang, Zhang, Mousaei, Xu, Geng, Scherer, Shi
arXiv:2504.10334 · 2025
Flying Hand

Decide at the end-effector. Let the platform be someone else’s problem.

Inspired by the success of robot learning in tabletop manipulation, we propose a unified aerial manipulation framework with an end-effector-centric interface that decouples high-level platform-agnostic decision-making from task-agnostic low-level control.

This was the starting point of Meeting 025. A fully actuated hexarotor, a 4-DoF arm, and an end-effector whole-body MPC, with teleoperation or imitation learning on top. It shows that the high level can talk only about the end-effector. FA-Hexa, the platform used here, is the same class of vehicle. The high level here goes further: a language-conditioned VLA stays on the arm, and flight itself is learned by a separate light module, rather than being only an MPC tracking an end-effector target.

Zhan, Jiang, Zhang, Geng
arXiv:2609.30521 · 2026
Aerial Manipulation in the Wild

A diffusion policy proposes the end-effector. Whole-body MPC flies it.

A Diffusion Policy is trained from manipulation demonstrations to generate desired end-effector motions from onboard observations. These learned commands are executed by a whole-body MPC that jointly coordinates the aerial platform and manipulator to realize the desired end-effector trajectory.

Same separation of a learned command and a model-based whole-body tracker, already outdoors with onboard LiDAR-inertial odometry, on a peg-in-hole task. The policy is still a Diffusion Policy trained from demonstrations. What changes here is the upper model: a VLA pretrained at tabletop scale, not a policy that has to learn the end-effector trajectory from aerial demonstrations alone.

Tucker, Liu, Swann, Ren, Yu, Sun, Kim, McGranahan, Vuong, Schwager
Stanford / Physical Intelligence
arXiv:2603.25038
π, But Make It Fly · AirVLA

Vision transfers. Writing flight into the VLA sampler is the part this project leaves out.

We find that while visual representations transfer effectively, the specific control dynamics required for flight do not. To bridge this “dynamics gap” without retraining the foundation model, we introduce a Payload-Aware Guidance mechanism that injects payload constraints directly into the policy’s flow-matching sampling process.
Our inference-time intervention, Payload-Aware Guidance, increases real-world pick-and-place task success from 23% to 50%.

AirVLA is the evidence that visual features move and flight dynamics do not, and it is also the design this project does not copy. Payload guidance is a physical constraint written into the same sampling process that produces the manipulation actions. That is the unified-chunk intervention above: flight feasibility edits the VLA’s own samples, and the coupling lives inside that backbone. Real pick-and-place rose from 23% to 50%, and a long-horizon compositional task reached 62%, with Gaussian-splatting navigation data. The lesson taken here is the dynamics gap. The mechanism is different: flight intent comes from a separate module and enters only as a condition.

Chen, Liu, Qian, et al. · Peking University / BAAI
arXiv:2507.01961
AC-DiT · Project page

Extract base motion first, then condition the body.

Since the motion of the mobile base directly influences the manipulator’s actions, we introduce a mobility-to-body conditioning mechanism that guides the model to first extract base motion representations, which are then used as context prior for predicting whole-body actions.

Fflight comes from this mechanism. The difference is in the lower-right of the pipeline figure. AC-DiT still predicts the whole body with one network, and a full training run is about two weeks on 8×A100, which is out of scope. The module kept here is only the first half: a small model that makes the base motion explicit, then hands that intent to a VLA that already knows the arm.

05 — Current works

Current works

Not working as mapping only

Frozen π0.5 is already in the loop. It flies forward. The current experiment is a closed-loop transfer from π0.5-DROID to a UA-Quad. Official weights stay frozen and are served over a WebSocket. A virtual Franka sits in between. The policy believes it is moving a 7-DoF arm over a table. The simulator is flying a quadrotor with a 4-DoF arm, and the target is on a wall. This is the arm policy before any flight module exists, which is why the task semantics are still missing. This zero-shot run was recorded on UA-Quad on 12 October. The project platform has since moved to FA-Hexa, and the specialist dataset on the Dataset page is recorded on FA-Hexa.

  1. 1The policy receives two RGB images, 7 joint angles, gripper state, and language. The instruction in this run is “press the button.”
  2. 2It returns a 15-step chunk. Each step is 7 joint velocities plus one gripper command. The first 8 steps run; steps 9–15 are dropped. Eight simulator ticks are 1/15 s, then the policy is queried again.
  3. 3Gripper: g > 0.5 maps to closed, otherwise open. The end-effector target is linearly interpolated over 8 ticks, with fixed orientation, no roll about the approach axis, and lead limited to 25 cm.
  4. 4A script can fly to 0.4 m in front of the button, reset the virtual Franka, and then hand over. The long transit does not have to be learned by the zero-shot VLA.

01 — Why audit the specialists

The LoRA learns whatever the specialist does. So every specialist was watched first.

π0.5 LoRA is trained on successful demonstrations from AM-Bench’s scripted specialists, one per task. A demonstration that passes a loose success check, or a specialist that fails for a reason unrelated to the task, goes straight into the training data. Before recording at scale, every task was recorded five times and watched, on the old platform (Demo 1) and again after the upgrades (Demo 2).

05 — Limits, and what comes next
Known limits

What the upgrades do not fix

  • Open door: the handle bar in door.usd sits flush on the door and has no collision body, so it cannot be gripped. “Using the handle” means closing the gripper at the handle position to unlatch, not grasping the bar.
  • Frame assembly changes finger stiffness and the forward limit for that task only.
  • The upgrades are on the AM-Bench branch omnihexa-task-fixes and are not committed yet.
  • Harvest lemon keeps one rare failure, 1 in 11 on FA-Hexa.
Next

Record at scale, then LoRA

  • Record 80 successful demonstrations per task with these specialists, the count AM-Bench used for π0.5, with small waypoint noise.
  • Keep the end-effector camera, as in the paper, and also record the base camera for a camera ablation.
  • Export to OpenPI at 20 Hz, compute norm stats, and run π0.5 LoRA on one 24 GB card.
